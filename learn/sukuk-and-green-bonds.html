Sukuk and Green Bonds

Government borrowing tied to something real — roads you can drive on, or projects that clean up after us. ₦10,000 gets you in.
A large number of Nigerians will not touch an ordinary bond, and not because they doubt the government. Charging or earning interest is forbidden in Islam. For years that meant the safest investments in the country were closed to millions of people. Sukuk is the answer to that, and it turned out to be popular far beyond the people it was designed for.

Lending, without lending

A Sukuk is often called an Islamic bond, which is close enough to start with and slightly wrong. The difference is the mechanism, and the mechanism is the lesson.

With an ordinary bond you lend money and are paid interest for the use of it. With a Sukuk you do not lend at all. Your money is used to build something real — a road, a bridge — and you become part-owner of that asset. The government then leases it back from you, and pays you rent.

The money arrives the same way at the same times. But it is rent on a thing you part-own, not interest on a loan, which is what makes it permissible under Islamic finance rules.

And because every naira is tied to a specific project, you can go and look at what your money built. Nigeria's Sukuk issues have been ring-fenced for roads and bridges across all six geopolitical zones and the FCT — 44 of them at the last count.

Bond vs Sukuk

Bond: "Lend us ₦100,000. We will pay you interest and return it." Sukuk: "Put ₦100,000 towards this road. You own a share of it. We will pay you rent to use it, then buy your share back." Same money in, same money out, entirely different reasoning.

What it pays, and who is actually buying it

The rental rate is fixed at issue and paid twice a year. The May 2025 issue — the seventh — ran for seven years at 19.75% a year.

Put that next to the other government options and it stands out. Read it carefully though: that rate was fixed in 2025, when rates across the country were higher. A Sukuk issued today would be priced against today's market, not that one. What the number tells you is that Sukuk is priced to compete, not that 19.75% is on offer now.

The reception makes the point better than any argument. The government asked for ₦300 billion. It received ₦2.2 trillion — more than seven times what it wanted, from retail investors, pension funds, conventional banks and non-interest banks alike.

Most of that money was not religiously motivated. It was people who wanted a government-backed return and found this one attractive. You do not have to be Muslim to buy a Sukuk, in exactly the way you do not have to be a farmer to buy from a farmer.

Green Bonds, and the honest caveat

A Green Bond works exactly like an ordinary FGN bond, with one restriction: the money may only be spent on projects with an environmental benefit — solar power, afforestation, clean transport. Nigeria was the first African country to issue one.

The mechanism is not new. What you are buying is the *promise about where the money goes*, and that is worth understanding clearly.

Here is the honest caveat: a green bond generally does not pay you more for being green. You are lending to the same government at roughly the same rate, with a condition attached about spending. If that condition matters to you, it is a real reason to choose one. If you expected a better return for it, there usually is not one.

That is not a criticism — it is simply what the product is, and knowing it means you are choosing rather than being sold to.

Getting in, tax, and getting out

The minimum is ₦10,000, then in multiples of ₦1,000. Slightly above the savings bond's ₦5,000, and far below the ₦50 million of the main bond auction.

Where: the same places as any government security — a stockbroker, your bank's investment desk, or an investment app. Offers are announced on dmo.gov.ng and run for a set window, exactly like the savings bond.

Tax: none. These are Federal Government securities, so the income is exempt under the Nigeria Tax Act 2025. The full rental payment reaches you.

Getting out early: both are listed on the Nigerian Exchange and can in principle be sold before maturity. Trading is thinner than for ordinary FGN bonds, so expect to wait, and expect the price on the day to be whatever the day says. Seven years is a long commitment — treat the term as real.

One warning. Offers are occasional, not monthly. There may be one a year, or none. Yield lists them on the Calendar tab when they are announced, which is the only reliable way to catch a window that does not repeat on a schedule.
