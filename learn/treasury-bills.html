Treasury Bills

The government’s short-term borrowing — and the one investment that never pays you a naira of interest, yet still pays you.
Here is a question that catches almost everybody. A Treasury Bill pays about 16.3% a year. It also never, at any point, pays you interest. Both of those are true, and understanding why is the moment Treasury Bills stop being confusing.

The one that pays you backwards

A Treasury Bill is the government borrowing for less than a year — 91 days, 182 days or 364 days.

With a savings bond, the government keeps your money and pays you along the way. With a Treasury Bill it does something simpler: it sells you a promise for less than the promise is worth.

You pay, say, ₦86,300 today. In 364 days the government pays you ₦100,000. No quarterly payments, no interest, nothing in between. The ₦13,700 difference is everything you earn, and you earn it by having bought the thing cheap.

This is called buying at a discount, and it is the entire mechanism.

A 364-day bill at 15.89%, September 2026’s rate

Today you pay → about ₦86,300 In 364 days you receive → ₦100,000 Your gain → ₦13,700 Nothing arrives in between. The whole return is the gap.

Why the price is the rate

Because the return is built into the price, the price is the only thing that moves. The ₦100,000 at the end never changes.

So when you hear that "Treasury Bill rates went up", what actually happened is that the government started selling those bills cheaper. A bill that cost ₦86,300 last month might cost ₦84,000 this month. Same ₦100,000 at the end, bigger gap, higher rate.

This is also why you may see two different numbers for the same auction. The gap as a share of what you *paid* is the true return; the gap as a share of the ₦100,000 *face value* is a smaller number, and it is the one usually quoted as the "stop rate". Neither is dishonest. They are answering different questions, and it is worth knowing which one you are being shown.

Same bill, two prices

Pay ₦86,300, receive ₦100,000 → you made ₦13,700 on ₦86,300 = 15.9% Pay ₦84,000, receive ₦100,000 → you made ₦16,000 on ₦84,000 = 19.0% The government did not change what it pays. It changed what it charges.

The minimum, and the part nobody tells you

The auction itself has a minimum of ₦50 million. That is not a typo, and it is why the auction is not for you.

What happens instead is that banks and stockbrokers buy in bulk at the auction and sell smaller parcels on to their customers, usually from about ₦100,000. This is completely normal and is how most Nigerians hold bills.

But it means the rate you are offered is not the rate you read about in the news. The bank keeps a slice. If the auction cleared at 15.89% and your bank offers you 13%, the difference is their margin — which may be fair for the service, or may not.

So ask two questions, in this order: *what rate am I getting?* and *what did the last auction clear at?* Yield puts the second number on the Calendar tab. Simply knowing it changes the conversation.

Tax, and getting out early

Tax: 10%, and this is new. Until recently, returns on Treasury Bills were tax-free, and a great deal of writing still online says so. Since a FIRS notice of 28 October 2025, 10% withholding tax is deducted from interest on short-term securities, Treasury Bills among them. On the ₦13,700 example that is ₦1,370, leaving you ₦12,330.

Note the contrast with the last lesson: Federal and State government *bonds* remain fully exempt, while *bills* no longer are. Same borrower, different tax, purely because of the length of the loan.

Getting out early: easier than a savings bond. Treasury Bills trade actively, so your bank or broker can usually sell yours before maturity. What you get depends on rates that day — if rates have risen, buyers want a bigger discount and you get less than you hoped.

A 91-day bill is short enough that most people simply wait.

What you actually keep

Gap on a 364-day bill → ₦13,700 Less 10% withholding tax → −₦1,370 What reaches you → ₦12,330 Still comfortably ahead of a savings account at 7.46%, which on ₦86,300 would have paid about ₦6,400 before any tax.

Check yourself

A friend says: "I bought a Treasury Bill for ₦900,000 and they gave me ₦1,000,000 after a year, but they never paid me any interest — I think I was cheated." What went wrong?

  • He was cheated. There should have been interest payments as well
  • Nothing. The ₦100,000 gap was the interest — that is how bills work
  • He should have received interest and the ₦900,000 back, so ₦1,000,000 is short
Show the answer

Exactly. He made ₦100,000 on ₦900,000 — about 11% — and he made it the only way a bill ever pays: by buying below face value. There is no separate interest payment to be missing, because the discount was the interest all along.
