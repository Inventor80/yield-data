Shares, and what an IPO really is

Owning a piece of a business instead of lending to one. Nothing is promised — which is the whole point, in both directions.
Everything so far has been lending. You hand over money, someone promises to give it back with extra, and the promise is the product. Shares are a different thing entirely, and the difference is not a matter of degree. You stop being a lender and become an owner.

From lender to owner

A share is a piece of a company. Buy one and you own a genuine fraction of that business — its factories, its contracts, its profits and its problems.

Nobody promises you anything. There is no coupon, no maturity date, nobody who owes you your money back. What you own is a claim on whatever the business turns out to be worth.

That sounds alarming after three lessons of guaranteed payments, and it should. But it cuts both ways: a bond can never pay you more than it promised, and a share has no ceiling at all. The NGX All-Share Index rose 51% in 2025. It also fell 45.8% in 2008. Both of those are the same feature.

Lending vs owning

Bond: "Lend me ₦100,000, I will pay you 15% and return it in 3 years." Share: "Own 0.0001% of this company. Whatever it is worth, that is what you have." One is a promise. The other is a stake.

The two ways a share pays you

Shares pay in two separate ways, and people routinely forget the first one.

Dividends. When a company makes a profit it may hand some of it to owners, usually once or twice a year. If you own 1,000 shares and the company declares a dividend of ₦3.50 a share, ₦3,500 arrives in your account. You did not sell anything. This is the closest thing to a coupon that shares have — except it is not promised, and a bad year can see it cut to nothing.

The price going up. If you paid ₦40 for a share and it is worth ₦55 later, you are ₦15 better off on each one. But that gain is only real when you sell; until then it is a number on a screen that can go back down.

Many Nigerian shares are held mainly for the first. A steady bank or cement company paying a reliable dividend can behave a little like a bond that might grow — while never actually promising anything.

1,000 shares bought at ₦40 each — ₦40,000

Year 1 dividend of ₦3.50 a share → ₦3,500 paid to you Year 2 dividend of ₦4.00 a share → ₦4,000 paid to you Price moves from ₦40 to ₦55 → your holding is worth ₦55,000 Sold then: ₦55,000 back, plus ₦7,500 already collected. If the price had fallen to ₦30 instead, the dividends would still have been paid.

What an IPO actually is

Most companies start out privately owned — by a founder, a family, a few investors. An IPO, or *initial public offering*, is the moment a company sells shares to the public for the first time. "Going public" and "listing" mean the same thing.

Two things happen at once. The company gets a large amount of money to build with, and ordinary people get the chance to own a piece of it at a set price, before it is traded freely.

The offer runs for a fixed window — a few weeks — and it works differently from ordinary buying:

The price is fixed. Everyone pays the same, however much they buy. You are not negotiating.

You apply; you are not guaranteed. If more money comes in than shares available, the offer is *oversubscribed*, and you receive fewer shares than you asked for. The rest is refunded. Getting some is normal; getting all is not.

Then it lists, and the price moves. Once trading opens, the price is whatever buyers and sellers agree. It may open above the offer price. It may open below. Nobody knows which, and anyone who tells you otherwise is guessing.

A rights issue is the same machinery for a company already listed, offering more shares to people who already own some.

How an offer works

1. The company publishes a prospectus: the price, the number of shares, what the money is for 2. The window opens, usually for a few weeks 3. You apply through a broker, an investment app, or NGX Invest, and pay 4. The window closes. Allotment decides who gets how many 5. Any money for shares you did not get is refunded 6. The shares list, and trade freely from then on

Getting in, tax, and getting out

What you need: a CSCS account — the central register of who owns which Nigerian shares. Your broker or investment app opens one for you, usually in a day or two, and you need your BVN and ID. You do this once, and it works for everything afterwards.

The minimum is set by each offer. Dangote Refinery's 2026 IPO asked for 10 shares at ₦525 — ₦5,250. On the ordinary market you can often start with a few thousand naira.

Tax changed in January 2026. Dividends have 10% withheld, and for most individuals that settles it. Gains when you sell shares are now taxed at your personal income tax rate rather than a flat rate, with reliefs that keep small retail investors out of it. Shares you already held before 2026 have their cost measured from the end of 2025, so earlier gains are not caught.

Getting out is the easiest of any instrument here: shares in an actively traded company can be sold on any trading day, with the money usually reaching you within a few days. There is no term to wait out. What you cannot control is the price on the day you need it — which is precisely why Stage 1 said money with a deadline does not belong in shares.

Before any offer, check the firm. Anyone taking your money must be registered with the Securities and Exchange Commission, and the register is public at sec.gov.ng. This takes two minutes and is the single most useful habit in this app.

Check yourself

An IPO is priced at ₦525 a share. You apply for 100 shares and send ₦52,500. The offer is heavily oversubscribed and you are allotted 40 shares. What happens next?

  • You lose the other ₦31,500 — that is the risk of applying
  • You are refunded ₦31,500, and own 40 shares worth ₦21,000 at the offer price
  • You must buy the remaining 60 shares when it lists
Show the answer

Correct. Money for shares you were not allotted is returned to you — that is standard in a public offer, not a favour. You end up owning 40 shares, and from the listing day their value is whatever the market says, which may be more or less than the ₦525 you paid.

Do this now

Two things, and the first costs nothing.

One: look up any Nigerian company you already buy from — a bank, a cement maker, a food company — and find its share price and dividend history. Seeing that the business you queue at every week is something you could own a piece of makes this concrete in a way no lesson does.

Two: if you are considering an offer, open sec.gov.ng and check that whoever is handling your money is on the register. Do it before you are in a hurry, because being in a hurry is the condition every bad decision is made in.
