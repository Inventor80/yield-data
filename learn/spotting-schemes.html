How to spot a scheme

Nigerians lost an estimated ₦911 billion to Ponzi schemes between 2000 and 2023. They were not stupid. Here is what they were shown.
You have just spent four lessons learning that inflation is eating your savings, that safe is not safe, and that starting early changes everything. That is exactly the frame of mind in which people hand their money to the wrong person. Motivated and in a hurry is the condition every bad decision is made in — so this lesson comes before the one about starting, not after it.

Why this lesson comes now

Between 2000 and 2023, Nigerians lost an estimated ₦911 billion to Ponzi schemes, on the NDIC's figures. In March 2025 the SEC published a list of 58 operating in the country at once.

The people who lost that money were not fools. They were teachers, traders, civil servants and graduates, and many were introduced by someone they had every reason to trust — a brother, a pastor, a colleague who had genuinely been paid.

That last part is the engine. A Ponzi scheme pays early members with the money of later members. For a while, everybody is paid, on time, exactly as promised. The payments are real, the screenshots are real, and the friend telling you it works is being honest about their own experience.

It collapses when new money stops arriving faster than withdrawals — and it always does, because it must. There is no investment underneath. There never was.

Why the early payments prove nothing

Month 1: 100 people join. 10 are paid, from the 90’s money. Month 2: 500 join. More are paid, loudly. Month 3: 2,000 join, on the strength of that. Month 4: growth slows. Withdrawals are delayed. Month 5: accounts are frozen. The last and largest group loses everything. That group is nearly always the majority.

The five signs, in the order you will meet them

1. A high return, stated as certain. This is the one that matters most, and Stage 1 already gave you the tool to judge it. The Federal Government pays around 16.3% to borrow. A solid company pays a few points more, because it is riskier. Anyone promising 30% a month is claiming to have found something the entire Nigerian financial system has missed. Guaranteed and high do not go together. In real investing, the higher the return, the less certain it is — that is what risk means.

2. You are paid for recruiting. A real investment pays you from what it earns. If a meaningful part of your return depends on bringing in other people, your return *is* other people, and you are inside the machine rather than investing through it.

3. Urgency. The window closes Friday. The price doubles next week. Only 50 slots left. Urgency exists to stop you checking, because checking is the one thing that ends the conversation.

4. It is vague about what it actually does. "Forex and crypto arbitrage using AI." Ask exactly who pays the return, out of what activity. A real answer names a borrower, an asset or a business. CBEX presented itself as the "China Beijing Equity Exchange" — a name assembled to sound like an institution.

5. It is not on the SEC register. Everything else is judgement. This one is a fact you can check in two minutes.

How CBEX actually ran, 2025

Promised → up to 100% in 30 days Paid → in early months, reliably Grew → through referral bonuses Then → withdrawals delayed Then → a “verification fee” to unlock your own account Then → accounts frozen, offices empty, protests in Lagos and Ibadan Every step was visible before the last one. The pattern was the same as MMM, Twinkas and Racksterli before it.

The two-minute check, and the sentence that saves you

Anybody who takes your money to invest it must be registered with the Securities and Exchange Commission, and the register is public at sec.gov.ng. Search the company name. If it is not there, that is the end of it — not a reason to ask more questions.

Two traps to know. First, a registered name is not a blanket endorsement: check that the entity you are dealing with is the one registered, and for the activity concerned. Second, CAC registration is not SEC registration. Almost anyone can register a company at the CAC; schemes wave that certificate precisely because it looks official to someone who has not met the distinction.

Then there is the sentence. When you are being pressed, by someone who means well, in a room where everyone else already agreed:

"Let me check the SEC register and I'll come back to you tomorrow."

It is polite, it is final, and it cannot be argued with. Watch what happens to the person's manner when you say it — because that reaction, more than anything on the slide deck, is your answer.

And if you are already in one: stop adding money today, withdraw what you can, and report it to the SEC and the police. Under the Investment and Securities Act 2025, running one carries prison terms of up to ten years. Recovering money is hard and often impossible; telling someone stops other people joining behind you.

Check yourself

Your cousin has been paid ₦40,000 a month for four months by a platform and shows you the alerts. He is genuinely happy and wants you in. What does his experience prove?

  • That it works — four months of real payments is strong evidence
  • Nothing about whether it is real. Early members always get paid; that is how the scheme grows
  • That it is risky but probably fine if you withdraw early
Show the answer

Exactly. Your cousin is telling the truth and the money arrived — and in a Ponzi scheme that is precisely what the early stage looks like, because he is being paid out of the deposits of people who joined after him. The evidence that matters is not whether anyone has been paid. It is where the money comes from, and whether the operator is on the SEC register.

Do this now

Go to sec.gov.ng and find the register of capital market operators. Search for any platform you have heard advertised — one you are not thinking of joining. Do it while nothing is at stake, so you know exactly where to look when something is.

Then do the useful thing: tell one person in your family what you just learned about early payments proving nothing. The people who lose most in these schemes are usually the ones who joined last, on the word of someone they loved. One conversation is worth more than anything else in this lesson.
