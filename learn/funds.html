Funds — when someone else does the work

Put ₦1,000 in a pot with thousands of other people and pay a professional to run it. The most accessible investment in Nigeria, and the most misunderstood.
Everything so far has needed a decision from you: which bond, which term, which company, which week. For most people that is the barrier — not the money, the deciding. A fund exists for exactly that problem, and you can start one with ₦1,000.

The pot

A mutual fund is a pot of money. Thousands of people put money in; a licensed fund manager invests the whole pot; everyone owns a slice of whatever it holds.

Your slice is measured in units. If the pot is worth ₦100 million and you own ₦100,000 of it, you own 0.1% of everything inside — and when the pot grows, your units are worth more.

The point is that ₦5,000 on its own buys you one thing. ₦5,000 inside a ₦100 million pot buys you a fraction of *everything the pot holds* — dozens of bonds, or dozens of companies. That is diversification, and it is the only thing in investing that genuinely reduces risk without reducing your expected return. A single company can fail. Forty of them failing at once is a different kind of event.

What ₦5,000 buys

On your own → one small holding in one thing Inside a fund → a sliver of everything the fund owns, managed daily by someone whose job it is Same ₦5,000.

The four kinds, and what each is actually for

The word "fund" covers products that behave nothing alike. What matters is what is in the pot.

Money market funds hold Treasury Bills, bank deposits and short-term corporate paper. They are the closest thing to a savings account that actually keeps up — you can usually withdraw within a few working days, and there is no fixed term. This is where most first-time Nigerian investors start, and reasonably so.

Fixed income or bond funds hold bonds, mostly government. Steadier income, slower growth, and the price moves with interest rates in the way Lesson 3 described.

Equity funds hold shares. No promises, bigger swings, and the most room to grow over long periods. Nigerian equity funds had an extraordinary run into 2026 — some returned over 50% in a year. Read that as evidence of how far they swing, not as a forecast.

Balanced funds hold a mixture, and try to be a whole portfolio in one product.

The question is never "is this a good fund" but "is this the right *kind* of fund for money I need in two years".

Matching the fund to the money

Money you may need within a year → money market fund 2 to 3 years → fixed income fund 5 years or more → equity fund, or balanced The same rule as Stage 1, Lesson 2. Time horizon picks the tool.

The fee, and the sentence you must be able to say

Nobody manages your money for free. A fund charges a management fee, taken as a percentage of the pot each year — often between 1% and 2%. It is deducted before the return you see is calculated, so it never appears as a line on your statement. You do not pay it; it is simply already gone.

On ₦100,000 at 1.5%, that is ₦1,500 a year. Small. Over twenty years, on a growing balance, it is not small at all — this is Stage 1's compounding lesson working against you.

The fee is not a scandal. Someone is doing real work, and a good manager can earn it several times over. But you should be able to say this sentence about anything you own: *"I am paying X% a year, for Y."* If you cannot fill in both blanks, you do not know what you have bought.

One thing a fund is not. A quoted return is what the fund *did*, not what it will do. Unless the document says the word guaranteed — and for anything holding shares it will not — last year's 50% is history, not an offer. Any salesperson who presents a past return as what you will get is either careless or selling you something. Both are reasons to leave.

Getting in, tax, and getting out

The minimum is the lowest of anything in this stage — commonly ₦1,000 to ₦5,000, through an app, in a few minutes. Many funds also let you add a set amount monthly, which suits a salary better than saving up a lump sum does.

Where: SEC-registered fund managers and the apps that distribute them. Check the SEC register at sec.gov.ng before you send money. This is the category with the most look-alikes, because "fund" sounds official and costs nothing to print on a website. A real fund manager is licensed, and the licence is public.

Tax depends on what is in the pot, because the fund pays it before your return is worked out: government bonds inside are exempt, Treasury Bills and corporate paper have 10% withheld, and dividends on shares have 10% withheld as a final tax for most individuals. The return quoted to you is normally after this has happened — but ask, because it is the difference between two funds that look identical.

Getting out is usually the easiest of any instrument here: you request a withdrawal and the money arrives within a few working days. Watch for two things — an exit fee if you withdraw within the first few months, and the fact that for an equity fund the price on the day you leave is whatever the market says that morning.

Check yourself

An app advertises a fund that "returned 52% last year". You have ₦300,000 you will need in eight months for rent. Is this a good fit?

  • Yes — 52% is far better than any bond, so it is the obvious choice
  • No. That return means it holds shares, which can be down when your rent is due
  • Yes, as long as you withdraw before the market falls
Show the answer

Right. A return like that comes from shares, and what swings up 52% can swing down. With a fixed deadline eight months away you cannot wait for a recovery. A money market fund would pay you far less and give you the one thing this money actually needs: being there in eight months. The third answer is the trap — nobody can time that, including professionals.

Do this now

Pick any fund you have heard advertised and find two numbers: its management fee and what it actually holds. Both are in its fact sheet, and if you cannot find them in five minutes, that is itself the answer.

Then check the manager's name on the SEC register at sec.gov.ng.

Do this once, on a fund you are not planning to buy. Doing it calmly is how the habit forms, and the habit is what protects you when something urgent and exciting is put in front of you.
