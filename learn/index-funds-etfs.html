Owning the whole market at once

Instead of choosing which company will do well, buy a slice of all of them. The idea that beat most professionals — and its awkward fit in Nigeria.
Every year, most professional fund managers fail to beat the market average. Not through laziness — they are clever, well resourced, and doing it full time. That result is so consistent, in so many countries, that it produced an uncomfortable question: if the experts cannot reliably beat the average, why is anyone paying to try?

Giving up on picking winners

An index is a list of companies and a way of tracking their combined value. The NGX All-Share Index is one: it tracks the whole Nigerian market. When the news says shares rose 3%, that is an index talking.

An index fund does something almost stupidly simple. It buys every company in the index, in proportion, and then does nothing. It makes no judgements, holds no opinions, and never tries to pick a winner. It just is the market.

An ETF — exchange-traded fund — is usually an index fund you buy and sell on the stock exchange like a share, at any moment during trading, rather than by filling in a form with a fund manager.

Two consequences follow, and they are the entire case.

It is cheap. Nobody is researching anything, so the fee is a fraction of an actively managed fund. Lesson 6 showed what a fee does over decades.

You are guaranteed the average. That sounds like a modest ambition until you remember that most professionals fall short of it.

Two ways to own Nigerian shares

Active fund → a manager picks the companies, charges more, may beat the market or may not Index fund → owns all of them, charges little, gets the market return minus a small fee One is a bet on skill. The other is a decision not to bet.

What is actually available here

There are twelve ETFs listed on the Nigerian Exchange, holding about ₦18 billion between them at the end of 2025 — up from ₦12.8 billion across eleven funds the year before.

Growing, and small. Be clear-eyed about that: ₦18 billion is the whole Nigerian ETF market, which in a large market would be one modest fund.

What exists covers more ground than most people expect:

Broad market funds tracking the biggest Nigerian companies — the Vetiva VG 30 is the largest by some distance.

A halal fund, the Lotus Halal ETF, holding only companies that meet Islamic investment rules — no conventional banks, no alcohol, no interest-based income.

A gold ETF, holding gold rather than companies at all.

Minimums are set in units rather than naira, so they vary: some let you buy a single unit, others ask for a thousand.

The awkward part, said plainly

The index argument was built in markets with thousands of companies, where no individual can hope to know them all. Nigeria is not that market, and the differences matter.

The index is concentrated. A handful of very large companies drive much of the NGX All-Share Index. Buying "the whole market" here gives you far less spread than the phrase suggests, and a bad year for one giant is a bad year for your fund.

Thin trading cuts both ways. A small ETF can have days where few units change hands, so the price you get may be further from the fund's true value than you would like — in either direction.

And the honest long view. Stage 1 said it and the Compare tool shows it: over ten years to 2025 the All-Share Index roughly matched inflation, and over twenty it lost to it. Owning the whole Nigerian market has not been a reliable way to get ahead of rising prices over long stretches. Over the last five years it was, substantially. Both are true, and which one you believe repeats is a genuine judgement, not a fact.

Tax is the same as for shares: 10% withheld on dividends, and gains taxed at personal income rates since January 2026, with reliefs for small investors.

So: a sensible, cheap way to own Nigerian shares if you want to own Nigerian shares. Not a shortcut past the question of whether you should.

Check yourself

An index fund promises only to match the market average, never to beat it. Why do so many people choose one anyway?

  • Because the average is usually higher than what most professional managers achieve, and it costs far less to get
  • Because index funds cannot lose money
  • Because the government guarantees index funds
Show the answer

Exactly. "Only the average" sounds modest until you see how many well-paid professionals finish below it — and the index fund charges a fraction of what they do, which compounds in your favour over decades. You give up the chance of beating the market in exchange for not paying to lose to it.

Do this now

Open the Compare tool and look at the NGX record underneath it — the yearly bars, and the 5, 10 and 20-year averages after inflation. That is the thing an index fund buys you, with nothing hidden.

Ask yourself honestly whether you would have held through 2018 and 2019, when the index fell two years running. If the answer is no, the number of years you can actually stay invested is smaller than you thought, and that — not the choice of fund — is the decision that matters.
