Lending to companies instead

Corporate bonds and commercial paper pay more than the government. Understanding exactly why is the difference between being paid for risk and being caught by it.
A company offers you 22% when the government is offering 16.3%. Your first instinct is that you have found something clever. Your second should be a question: why is this company paying six points more than the Federal Government for the same money?

Why anyone pays more than the government

There is always an answer, and it is never "because they are generous".

The government can tax, and in the last resort it prints the currency it owes you in. A company can do neither. It pays you out of a business that might have a bad year, lose a contract, or fail entirely.

So a company has to offer more than the government or nobody would lend to it. That extra is called the credit spread, and it is the market's price for the chance that you are not paid back.

This is the single most useful idea in fixed income: the extra yield is not a bonus, it is a payment for a risk you are accepting. Sometimes the payment is generous and the risk is small — that is a good investment. Sometimes the payment is large because the risk is enormous. The number alone cannot tell you which, and anyone who quotes you only the number is hiding the half that matters.

Reading a rate properly

Government, 364 days → about 16.3% A strong bank, 270 days → a little more A smaller company, 180 days → more again Someone you have never heard of → much more Each step up is the market saying: more can go wrong here.

Commercial paper — the short one

Commercial paper is a company borrowing for a short period, usually between 90 and 364 days. It works exactly like a Treasury Bill: you pay less than the face value and receive the full amount at maturity, and the gap is your return.

It has become popular in Nigeria because it sits in a useful gap — better paid than a Treasury Bill, shorter and simpler than a bond. The first commercial paper was listed on the Nigerian Exchange in February 2026, which made pricing visible in a way it had not been before.

The thing to understand is what it is not. Commercial paper is usually unsecured — there is no asset set aside for you if things go wrong. You are relying entirely on the company being able to pay on the day, and the shortness of the term is the main protection: it is easier to be confident about a company's next six months than its next six years.

So the question to ask is never "what does it pay". It is: *do I believe this company will still be comfortably paying its bills in nine months?*

Same shape, different borrower

Treasury Bill: pay ₦86,300 → receive ₦100,000 from the government Commercial paper: pay ₦84,000 → receive ₦100,000 from a company The extra ₦2,300 is what you are being paid to take the company’s word instead of the government’s.

Corporate bonds, and how to judge one

A corporate bond is the long version: a company borrowing for several years and paying a coupon along the way, exactly like an FGN bond.

You will not read a company's accounts before buying, and you are not expected to. But three things are quick and tell you most of what you need.

Who is the borrower, really? A large bank, a cement maker with a thirty-year history, a subsidiary you have never heard of with an impressive name. This one question does most of the work.

Is there a credit rating? Nigerian issues are usually rated by agencies such as Agusto & Co or GCR. It is a letter grade — A is stronger than B, and anything below investment grade is a warning written down. It is an opinion, not a guarantee, and ratings have been wrong before. It is still far better than guessing.

Is it secured, and against what? A secured bond has specific assets behind it. Unsecured means you are one of many people the company owes, if it comes to that.

And hold the answers next to the spread. A well-known bank paying two points over the government is a normal trade. An unknown company paying fifteen points over is not an opportunity everyone else has missed — it is the market telling you something, loudly.

Tax, getting out, and how much to risk

Tax: 10%. Both corporate bonds and commercial paper have 10% withheld on the interest, under the same FIRS treatment of short-term securities that now applies to Treasury Bills. This matters when you compare: a corporate bond at 20% and a government bond at 18% are much closer than they look, because the government one is exempt and the corporate one is not. Compare after tax, always.

Getting out early: harder than government paper. Corporate issues trade thinly, so selling before maturity may mean waiting or accepting a poor price. Assume you are holding to the end.

And the part that is not about the instrument at all. Government default is a remote possibility. Company default is an ordinary event that happens somewhere every year. The protection is not picking correctly every time — nobody does — it is size. One company's paper should never be so large a share of your money that its failure changes your life. A fund spreads this for you across dozens of borrowers, which is precisely what the fee in Lesson 6 buys.

Comparing honestly

FGN bond at 18%, tax exempt → you keep 18% Corporate bond at 20%, 10% withheld → you keep 18% Identical return. Only one of them can fail to pay you.

Check yourself

A company you have never heard of is offering commercial paper at 35% for 180 days, when Treasury Bills pay about 16.3%. How should you read that?

  • As an opportunity most investors have overlooked
  • As the market pricing in a high chance of not being repaid
  • As normal — companies always pay roughly double the government
Show the answer

Exactly. A spread that wide is not generosity or an oversight; it is the price the market demands to lend to that borrower, and it is set by people who looked at the accounts. High yield is compensation for risk, and the size of the compensation tells you the size of the risk. It might still pay in full — but you should know which bet you are making.

Do this now

The next time any investment is offered to you — by a bank, an app, a friend — do this before anything else: find the government rate for the same length of time. It is on the Calendar tab.

Subtract. The difference is what you are being paid to take on risk instead of lending to the government.

Then ask the person offering it one question: what is the risk I am being paid for? A good answer names the borrower and its position. A bad answer talks about the return again. That single question, asked calmly, filters out most of what will ever be pitched to you.
