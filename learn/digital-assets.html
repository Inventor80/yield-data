Crypto, and what the law now says

Nigeria brought digital assets under the SEC in 2025. What that changed, what it did not, and how to tell a licensed exchange from a scheme.
Nigeria is one of the most active crypto markets on earth, and a great many of the people reading this already hold some. Leaving it out of an app about investing would not protect anyone — it would just mean the only voices explaining it are the ones selling it.

Why this lesson exists

A digital asset is a token recorded on a shared ledger rather than by a bank. Bitcoin is the original; stablecoins such as USDT are designed to track the dollar and are what most Nigerians actually use, because they are a way to hold dollars rather than a bet on a price.

It is worth being precise about what you own, because the two are nothing alike:

A share is a piece of a company that earns money. A bond is a debt somebody owes you. A token is neither — it has no coupon, no profits, and nobody owes you anything. Its price is whatever the next person will pay.

That is not an argument against owning it. It is an argument against thinking of it as the same kind of thing as the rest of this stage.

What the law says now

This changed recently and most people have not caught up.

The Investments and Securities Act 2025 expanded the legal definition of securities to include virtual and digital assets. In plain terms: the SEC now regulates this, where before it was a grey area the CBN had tried to close off.

What that means in practice:

Exchanges and custodians must be licensed. Several Nigerian platforms have gone through the SEC's incubation programme toward full licensing; others have left the market. In January 2026 the minimum capital for a digital asset exchange was raised to ₦2 billion, with firms given until mid-2027 to comply — which will thin the field further.

Holding it yourself was never illegal, and the Act does not change that. It regulates the businesses, not your wallet.

Proposed, not yet law: in August 2026 the SEC published draft rules that would cap retail investment at ₦1 million per issuer and ₦10 million a year, with local incorporation requirements for platforms. Those were out for comment and may change — but the direction is clear, and it tells you how the regulator sees the risk.

Before and after

Before: no licence to get, no rules to break, no regulator to complain to. After: platforms must be licensed by the SEC, and an unlicensed one is now operating illegally rather than merely unofficially. Your own holdings: unchanged, and legal.

Telling a licensed platform from a scheme

This is the practical part, and it uses the same register as everything else.

Check sec.gov.ng. A platform taking Nigerians' money to trade digital assets should appear there, either licensed or in the SEC's incubation programme. Not appearing is the end of the conversation, however well known the brand is.

Learn the difference between an exchange and a scheme. An exchange lets you buy and sell at a market price and charges a fee. A scheme promises a return — "30% monthly on your USDT" — which no exchange can do, because an exchange does not know what the price will do either.

That distinction is the whole of it. Crypto going up is not a promise anyone can make, so anyone making it is not selling you crypto. Everything in Stage 1's lesson on spotting a scheme applies here with more force, because the losses in this category have been faster and larger. CBEX, which collapsed in 2025, promised up to 100% in thirty days and took deposits in USDT.

Tax, getting out, and how much

Tax. Gains are capital gains, and since January 2026 gains are taxed at your personal income rate with the reliefs for smaller investors described in Stage 1. The practical difficulty is records: unlike a broker, most platforms will not hand you a tidy annual statement, and the obligation to keep track is yours.

Getting out. Fast, in normal conditions — minutes on a licensed exchange. The qualifier matters: the moment everybody wants out at once is the moment withdrawals slow down, and "we are upgrading our systems" is the oldest sentence in this market.

How much. The honest answer is the same as the last lesson, more firmly. Money you could lose entirely without it changing anything. Not the emergency fund, not fees, not the deposit.

And one specifically Nigerian point. Many people here hold stablecoins not to speculate but to escape the naira — which is a real motive, and Stage 2's lesson on dollar assets covers it. If that is your reason, compare it with the regulated routes in that lesson: a domiciliary account, a dollar fund, an FGN Eurobond. They do the same job with a named institution behind them.

Same goal, four routes

Want to hold value in dollars? Domiciliary account → a bank, regulated, little or no interest Dollar mutual fund → SEC-registered manager, from small amounts FGN Eurobond → the federal government, pays interest in dollars Stablecoin → an exchange, no interest, no deposit protection

Check yourself

A platform offers "guaranteed 20% monthly on your USDT, backed by AI trading". It has thousands of users and pays reliably. What is it?

  • A legitimate high-yield crypto product
  • A scheme — no exchange can guarantee a return on an asset whose price it cannot predict
  • Risky but worth a small amount
Show the answer

Correct. The word "guaranteed" is the whole answer. An exchange earns fees; it has no idea what any price will do, so it cannot promise a return — and anyone promising one is paying old members with new deposits. That it pays reliably and has thousands of users describes every such scheme in its early months, including CBEX before it collapsed in 2025.

Do this now

If you hold anything on a platform, look it up on sec.gov.ng today. Licensed, in the incubation programme, or absent — find out which, before you need to know.

And if your reason for holding stablecoins is the naira rather than the price, read the dollar assets lesson in this stage. The regulated routes do the same job, and somebody answers the phone.
