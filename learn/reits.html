Property, without buying a building

Rent arrives, you own a share of the buildings, and you can sell on a Tuesday. What a REIT is, and why Nigeria has so few.
Ask Nigerians where they would put serious money and most say land or property. They are not wrong — it has built more family wealth here than any financial instrument. But it asks for millions up front, years of paperwork, a caretaker, tenants who do not pay, and the certainty that you cannot sell half a building in a hurry.

The Nigerian dream, and its problem

A REIT — a Real Estate Investment Trust — is a company that does nothing but own income-producing property: offices, shopping centres, flats. You buy units in the company, and you own a share of all of it.

The rent tenants pay flows into the REIT, costs come out, and most of what is left is paid to unit holders. That distribution requirement is what makes a REIT a REIT rather than an ordinary property company: it exists to pass rent through to you, not to sit on it.

So you become a landlord without being a landlord. No tenants to chase, no roof to fix, no agent, and none of the ₦40 million.

Two ways to own property

Buying a flat → millions up front, months of paperwork, one tenant, one location, sold in months if you are lucky Buying REIT units → from a few thousand naira, bought in minutes, spread across many buildings and tenants, sold on any trading day

How it actually pays you, and the tax quirk worth knowing

A REIT pays you in the same two ways shares do — a distribution from the rent, and whatever happens to the unit price — but the first is the point. People buy REITs for income.

Nigeria REIT, listed on the Nigerian Exchange, declared ₦1.72 a unit for holders on its July 2026 record date, paid the following September, after its profit rose sharply. Own 10,000 units and that is ₦17,200 before tax, for owning something you never had to visit.

The tax has a quirk that is genuinely in your favour. Under the Nigeria Tax Act 2025, a SEC-registered real estate investment company pays no company income tax provided it distributes at least 75% of its income within 12 months of its year end. Ordinary companies pay tax on profits and then you pay tax on the dividend — taxed twice. A qualifying REIT is taxed once: the distribution reaching you has 10% withheld, as dividends do, and that is the end of it.

That is why the 75% rule exists at all. The tax relief is the bribe for passing the rent through to you instead of keeping it.

10,000 units at ₦1.72

Declared → ₦17,200 Less 10% withholding → −₦1,720 Reaches you → ₦15,480 For property you do not manage, in buildings you have never entered.

The honest problem with Nigerian REITs

Everything above is the case for REITs. Here is the case against, and it is specific to Nigeria rather than to the idea.

There are very few. A handful are listed, against dozens in comparable markets. Little choice, and little competition to be good.

They trade thinly. Some days almost no units change hands. In theory you can sell on any trading day; in practice a large holding may take time, or a discount, to shift. The liquidity advantage over a physical building is real but smaller than the theory suggests.

The price can drift from the value of the buildings. Units often trade below what the underlying property is worth, sometimes for years. That can be an opportunity, and it can equally be the market's verdict on the management.

Property is not automatically safe. Empty offices pay no rent, and a REIT with vacancies cuts its distribution. Buildings are only as good as the tenants in them.

None of this makes REITs a bad idea. It makes them a small, income-producing part of a portfolio rather than the answer to the property question — and it means judging the specific REIT, not the category.

Check yourself

A REIT must distribute at least 75% of its income within 12 months to stay exempt from company income tax. Why should that matter to you as a buyer?

  • It does not — it is an accounting detail between the REIT and the tax authorities
  • It is what forces the rent through to unit holders, and it stops the income being taxed twice
  • It guarantees a minimum return on your units
Show the answer

Right on both halves. The rule is what obliges the REIT to pass rent to you rather than retain it, and the exemption means the money is taxed once — 10% withheld on your distribution — instead of at the company and again in your hands. It guarantees nothing about the amount: 75% of a poor year is still a poor year.

Do this now

Look up a Nigerian REIT and find two numbers: the distribution per unit it last declared, and the unit price. Divide the first by the second and you have the yield — what the rent pays you as a percentage of what you would pay to buy in.

Now hold that next to a Treasury Bill at 16.3%.

If the REIT pays less than the government for far more risk, that tells you something. If it pays more, the next question is why — vacancies, the buildings, the management. Either way you are now asking the question a professional would, and it took two minutes.
