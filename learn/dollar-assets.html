When the naira itself is the risk

A 16% return in a currency that lost 30% of its value is not a 16% return. What Nigerians can actually do about it.
Ask anyone who was saving in 2023 what happened to their money. They did nothing wrong, moved nothing, lost nothing to any scheme — and what their savings could buy abroad, or import, or pay school fees with, fell by more than half. Not because prices rose. Because the naira moved.

The question behind every Nigerian investment

Everything in this app so far has been measured in naira. A savings bond pays 14.071%, inflation is 15.39%, a Treasury Bill pays 16.3%. All true, and all measured with the same ruler.

But the ruler itself changes length. Devaluation is the naira buying less of other currencies than it did. It shows up first in anything imported — fuel, phones, medicine, a laptop, a plane ticket, school fees abroad.

So there are really two questions about any Nigerian investment, and most people only ask the first:

1. Did it beat inflation? — does it buy more here than it did?
2. Did it hold its value against other currencies? — does it buy more *there* than it did?

A year where you earn 16% and the naira falls 30% against the dollar is a year where you got richer in naira and poorer in dollars. Both are real. Which one matters depends entirely on what you are saving *for*.

Two people, same year, same return

Both earn 16% on ₦1,000,000. Amaka is saving for rent in Lagos. Her money grew and mostly kept up with prices. She is fine. Tunde is saving for his daughter’s fees in the UK. Those fees are in pounds. His 16% bought less sterling at the end of the year than his money would have bought at the start. The investment did not fail Tunde. It was the wrong currency for his goal.

What you can actually hold

There are four realistic routes, and they are not equally sensible.

A domiciliary account. An ordinary bank account that holds dollars instead of naira. It protects you from the naira moving, and that is all it does — most pay little or no interest, so inflation in dollars nibbles away quietly. Safety, not growth.

FGN Eurobonds. The same Federal Government you have been lending to all through this stage, borrowing in dollars instead. You are paid dollars, by the same borrower, so the *currency* risk is gone while the borrower stays the one you already know. Minimums are higher — often a few thousand dollars — though funds and apps now slice them smaller.

Dollar mutual funds. The fund structure from the last lesson, holding dollar assets. This is how most Nigerians access dollars sensibly: low minimums, professional management, and no need to find a Eurobond yourself.

Foreign shares. Apple, Microsoft, an S&P 500 index fund, through an SEC-registered app. Real ownership in a hard currency — and every bit as capable of falling as Nigerian shares, now with two things moving at once.

What each one is for

Domiciliary account → holding value, no growth Eurobond → dollar income, government-backed Dollar fund → the practical middle, from small amounts Foreign shares → long-term growth, real risk None of these is "safe". They swap one risk for another.

The part the adverts leave out

Dollar assets are sold in Nigeria as an escape hatch. They are not. They are a swap, and here is what you are swapping into.

You can be wrong in both directions. If the naira is stable or strengthens for a year, your dollar holdings lose value in naira while your neighbour's Treasury Bill quietly pays 16.3%. Currencies do not only move one way, even when it feels like it.

Dollars have their own inflation. Dollars sitting idle lose value too, just more slowly than most people assume.

Getting money in and out has friction. Conversion spreads, transfer fees, and rules that change. The rate you are quoted is rarely the rate you read about.

Regulation matters more here, not less. Foreign-facing platforms are exactly where unlicensed operators cluster, because "we invest in US stocks" sounds sophisticated. Check sec.gov.ng. Several of the well-known Nigerian dollar platforms are properly licensed; the point is that you should know which, rather than assume.

And the honest conclusion: this is not all-or-nothing. If part of your life is priced in foreign currency — fees, travel, imported goods, family abroad — then holding some of your money in that currency is not speculation, it is matching. If your whole life is priced in naira, a large dollar position is a bet, and it should be a small one.

Check yourself

Chidi earns in naira, spends in naira, and is saving for a plot of land in Ibadan in three years. A friend urges him to move everything into dollars. What should he weigh?

  • Move it all — dollars always beat the naira over time
  • His goal is priced in naira, so a large dollar position adds a risk his plan does not need
  • Keep it all in naira — dollar assets are speculation for everybody
Show the answer

Exactly right. The land costs naira. Holding dollars means that if the naira strengthens, he has less money for the same plot — a risk that buys him nothing, because his goal was never in dollars. Matching the currency to the goal is the whole idea. Had he been saving for fees abroad, the answer would flip completely.

Do this now

Write down what your savings are actually *for*, and next to each one, the currency it will be spent in. School fees abroad, a trip, an imported car, a laptop — those are foreign currency, whatever account they sit in today. Rent, land, school fees here — naira.

That list, and not a prediction about the exchange rate, is what tells you how much of your money belongs in dollars. Almost nobody does this, which is why the question usually gets answered by whoever spoke last.
