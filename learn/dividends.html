Dividends — getting paid to own

Money that arrives without selling anything. How it is decided, when you have to own the shares to get it, and what actually lands.
Everyone discusses share prices. Almost nobody discusses the money that quietly arrives from shares you never sold — and for a lot of ordinary Nigerian investors, that money is most of what they have actually received.

The payment nobody sells you

When a company makes a profit it has a choice: keep the money to grow the business, or hand some to the people who own it. The part handed over is a dividend.

It is quoted per share. A company declaring "₦3.50 per share" pays ₦3.50 for each share you hold, however many that is. Own 5,000 shares and ₦17,500 is declared for you.

This is not the company doing you a favour, and it is not a bonus. You own the business; that is your share of what it earned.

The important difference from everything earlier in this stage: a dividend is never promised. A coupon on a bond is contractual — miss it and the government or company is in default. A dividend is a decision, made afresh each year by the board. A good year can raise it. A bad year can cut it to nothing, and no rule has been broken.

Bond coupon vs dividend

Coupon: "We owe you 14.071%. Not paying is default." Dividend: "We earned well this year, so we are sharing ₦3.50 a share. Next year, we will see." One is a debt. The other is a decision.

The dates that decide whether you get paid

This is where people lose money through a misunderstanding, so it is worth being exact.

A dividend follows four steps.

Declaration. The board announces an amount, usually with the year's results.

The qualification date — also called the record date. The company takes a snapshot of its register. Whoever owns the shares on that day gets the dividend. Buy the day after and you get nothing, even if you hold for years afterwards.

Approval, at the annual general meeting.

Payment, into your bank account or as a direct credit, typically weeks after the qualification date. Nigeria REIT's July 2026 distribution reached holders in early September — that gap is normal.

Now the part that catches people. Around the qualification date the share price usually falls by roughly the dividend. This is not a crash and nobody is cheating you: the company is about to hand out cash, so it is worth slightly less than it was the day before.

Which kills the clever idea everyone has independently: buy just before the qualification date, collect the dividend, sell just after. You collect ₦3.50 and the price drops about ₦3.50. You have achieved a taxable event and some brokerage fees.

Why the quick trade does not work

Day before qualification → share worth ₦50.00 You buy. Dividend of ₦3.50 declared to you. Day after → share worth about ₦46.50 You hold ₦46.50 of share and ₦3.15 of dividend after tax. You started with ₦50.00.

What actually lands, and what the yield tells you

Tax: 10%, withheld before you see it. The registrar deducts it and pays you 90% of what was declared. For most individuals that settles the tax on that income — it is not a payment on account you top up later. So ₦17,500 declared is ₦15,750 received.

Always do that arithmetic before comparing a dividend with anything else. A 12% dividend yield is about 10.8% after tax; a government bond at 12% is 12%, exempt.

Dividend yield is the dividend divided by the share price — what the payment is worth relative to what you would pay to buy in. A ₦3.50 dividend on a ₦40 share is 8.75%.

It is genuinely useful and quietly treacherous, because the price is the bottom of the fraction. A yield can rise because the dividend went up, or because the price collapsed. The second is the more common reason for a strikingly high yield, and it usually means the market expects the dividend to be cut. A 20% yield is more often a warning than an offer.

So read a yield with the price history beside it, and ask whether the company has actually paid a similar amount for several years running. Consistency is the signal. A single large payment is not a pattern.

The same yield, two different stories

Company A: dividend ₦4.00, price ₦40 → 10%. Dividend rose from ₦3.20 last year. Company B: dividend ₦4.00, price ₦40 → 10%. Price was ₦80 a year ago. Identical number. Only one of them is good news.

Check yourself

A company declares a dividend with a qualification date of 12 March. You buy the shares on 14 March, two days later, and hold them for three years. What do you receive from this dividend?

  • The full dividend — you owned the shares when it was paid
  • Nothing. The register was taken on 12 March, and you were not on it
  • A part of it, worked out from the days you held the shares
Show the answer

Correct, and this is the detail that catches people. The register is a snapshot: own the shares on the qualification date or you are not paid, however long you hold afterwards. The consolation is that you almost certainly bought at a price already reduced by roughly the dividend you missed — and you will be on the register for the next one.

Do this now

Pick a Nigerian company you would plausibly own — a bank, a cement maker, a food company — and find its dividend for each of the last five years.

You are not looking for the biggest number. You are looking for whether it was paid every year, and whether it grew. Five straight years of a rising dividend tells you more about a business than almost any other single fact you can look up in ten minutes.

Then open the Dividend calculator and work out how many shares that company would take to produce ₦50,000 a year. That number is the honest distance between here and income from shares, and it is better to see it clearly than to imagine it.
