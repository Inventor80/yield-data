The FGN Savings Bond

The one designed for ordinary people. ₦5,000 gets you in, the government pays you every three months, and you know the rate before you commit.
Every month the Federal Government needs money it does not have yet — for roads, salaries, debt it already owes. It could borrow from banks and pension funds, and mostly it does. But one week a month it opens a window for everybody else. ₦5,000 is enough to walk through it.

You are the lender

An FGN Savings Bond is a loan you make to the Federal Government for two or three years.

That is the whole idea. You are not buying a share of anything, and nobody is managing your money. You hand over an amount, the government writes down that it owes you, and it pays you to wait.

The word bond just means the promise is written down and can be counted on. It is the same word as in "my word is my bond".

The deal, in one line

You lend ₦100,000 for 3 years. The government pays you interest every 3 months. At the end of the 3 years, you get your ₦100,000 back.

How it actually pays you

The rate is called the coupon, and it is fixed on the day you buy. If the rate is 14.071% and you put in ₦100,000, the government owes you 14.071% of ₦100,000 every year for the whole term — no matter what happens to rates afterwards.

That money does not pile up and arrive at the end. It is split into four and paid into your bank account every three months, which is why the FGN Savings Bond is one of the few investments in Nigeria that pays an ordinary person an income while they still own it.

The word "coupon" is a leftover from when bonds were paper certificates with little tear-off tabs around the edge. Every three months you tore one off and took it to the bank to be paid. The tabs are gone; the name stuck.

₦100,000 at 14.071%, October 2026’s 3-year rate

Every 3 months → ₦3,518 into your account Every year → ₦14,071 Over the full 3 years → ₦42,213 in interest Then at the end → your ₦100,000 back, in full You end with ₦142,213, having never had to do anything after the first phone call.

What it costs to get in, and where you go

The minimum is ₦5,000, then in steps of ₦1,000 after that. The maximum is ₦50 million, which is not a number most people need to worry about.

That ₦5,000 floor is deliberate. The DMO set it low precisely so that this is not an instrument only wealthy people can reach.

You cannot buy it at a bank counter, and this is where most people give up. Subscriptions go through a stockbroker appointed by the DMO as a distribution agent, or through your bank's investment or treasury desk — a different department from the one that opens current accounts. Several investment apps now handle it too, in about three minutes.

You need a bank account in your own name, your BVN and a valid ID. That is the entire list.

What actually happens

1. The DMO publishes the month’s circular with the rate 2. You tell a broker, a bank investment desk or an app how much you want 3. You pay before the window closes on Friday 4. Settlement happens the following week 5. Three months later, the first payment arrives

Tax, and getting out early

These are the two questions almost nobody answers, and they are the two that decide whether this suits you.

Tax: none. Interest on bonds issued by the Federal Government and by State Governments is fully exempt under the Nigeria Tax Act 2025. The ₦14,071 a year in the example above is ₦14,071 in your hand. This is genuinely unusual — Treasury Bills, commercial paper and corporate bonds all now have 10% withheld. Government bonds do not.

Getting out early: possible, but not simple. The bond is listed on the Nigerian Exchange, so in principle you can sell it to somebody else before it matures. In practice trading in savings bonds is thin, so you may wait, and you may have to accept less than you hoped. If rates have risen since you bought, your bond pays less than newer ones and is worth less; if rates have fallen, it is worth more.

So treat the term as real. Money you might need next year does not belong here — that is what Lesson 2 of Stage 1 was about.

Check yourself

You put ₦200,000 into a 3-year FGN Savings Bond at 14.071%. Six months later, the government announces new bonds paying 18%. What happens to your payments?

  • They rise to 18% — the government pays the current rate
  • They stay at 14.071% for your whole three years
  • The bond is cancelled and reissued at the new rate
Show the answer

Correct, and this is the whole point of a fixed coupon. It was fixed on the day you bought: ₦7,036 every three months for three years, whatever happens to rates. That cuts both ways — if rates had fallen to 10%, you would still be collecting 14.071% while everyone buying that month collected less. Certainty is what you are buying.

Do this now

Turn on the alert for the next window — it is linked just below.

Then make one phone call, today, before the window opens: ring your bank and ask "does your investment desk handle FGN Savings Bond subscriptions?" If the answer is no, ask a second bank, or look for a stockbroker on the DMO's list of distribution agents at dmo.gov.ng.

Doing this while nothing is open is the trick. The window is five working days; sorting out who you are buying through is what makes people miss it.
