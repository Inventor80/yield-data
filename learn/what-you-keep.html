What you actually keep

Every rate in this app is before tax and fees. Here is what reaches your account — and why two investments paying the same can leave you with different money.
Two people invest ₦1,000,000 for a year. Both are promised 18%. One ends the year with ₦180,000 of income, the other with ₦162,000. Neither was cheated, and both got exactly what was advertised.

The number on the poster is not the number in your account

Every rate you have seen so far — in this app, in a bank advert, in a broker's message — is a gross rate. It is what the investment pays before anything is taken off.

Two things come off, and they behave differently.

Tax is deducted by law, usually before you ever see the money. You rarely have to do anything; you simply receive less than the headline.

Fees are charged by whoever handles the money, and are often invisible in exactly the same way — subtracted before the return is calculated, so they never appear as a line item.

What matters is what lands. The habit worth building is small: when anyone quotes you a rate, ask *is that before or after tax*, and *what do you charge*. Most people never ask either.

Where the difference came from

Both were paid 18% on ₦1,000,000. One held an FGN bond → interest exempt → ₦180,000 kept The other held corporate paper → 10% withheld → ₦162,000 kept Same rate, same year, ₦18,000 apart. The only difference was who borrowed the money.

What is taxed, and what is not

Nigeria's tax rules changed on 1 January 2026, under the Nigeria Tax Act 2025. A great deal of advice still online describes the old position. Here is where things stand.

Government bonds — nothing. Interest on Federal and State Government bonds, including FGN Savings Bonds and Sukuk, is fully exempt. ₦14,071 declared is ₦14,071 received.

Treasury Bills and company debt — 10%. Since a FIRS notice of 28 October 2025, 10% is withheld on interest from short-term securities: Treasury Bills, commercial paper, corporate bonds. This is the change most people have not registered, because T-bills were tax-free for years.

Dividends — 10%, withheld before you see it. The registrar deducts it and pays you 90% of what was declared. For most individuals that is the end of it.

Gains on shares — most small investors are exempt. Since January 2026 gains are taxed at your personal income tax rate rather than a flat rate, *but* they are exempt where your total proceeds are under ₦150 million and your total gains under ₦10 million in any 12 months. Both conditions have to hold. For the amounts most Nigerians invest, this simply does not arise.

Pensions — the best treatment available. Contributions come out of pre-tax income, and withdrawals on retirement or from age 50 are exempt.

Two instruments, same 18%, one year, ₦1,000,000

FGN Savings Bond → ₦180,000, nothing withheld → ₦180,000 Treasury Bill → ₦180,000, less 10% → ₦162,000 Shares paying 18% in dividends → ₦180,000, less 10% → ₦162,000 Compare after tax, not before. It is the only comparison that is about your money.

Fees — the quiet one

Tax is at least announced. Fees are frequently not, and over long periods they do more damage.

Fund management fees, often 1% to 2% a year, are taken out of the pot before the return you are shown is worked out. You never pay them; they are already gone.

Brokerage commission applies when you buy or sell shares — a percentage each way.

The spread is the least visible of all: the gap between the rate an auction cleared at and the rate your bank offers you on the same Treasury Bill. Nobody calls it a fee. It is the largest one most savers pay.

Apply Stage 1's compounding lesson to a fee and it is uncomfortable. 1.5% a year sounds like nothing. Over twenty years, on a growing balance, it quietly removes a meaningful share of everything you were going to have — the same mechanism that was working for you, running in reverse.

None of this means fees are wrong. Someone is doing work, and a good fund can earn its fee several times over. It means you should know the number. If you cannot say what you are paying, you are not in a position to judge whether it is worth it.

Check yourself

Your bank offers a Treasury Bill at 13%. The Calendar tab shows the last auction cleared at 16%. What is the 3% difference?

  • Tax that the government takes
  • The bank’s margin — what it keeps for buying in bulk and selling you a small parcel
  • An error — the rates should match
Show the answer

Right. The bank bought at the auction, where the minimum is ₦50 million, and is selling you a smaller parcel at a lower rate. The gap is its margin, and it is entirely normal — the point is to know it exists, so you can ask whether another bank or broker offers better. The 10% withholding tax is a separate deduction, taken from the return you do receive.

Do this now

Take the investment where you currently have the most money — a savings account, a fixed deposit, a fund — and work out one number: what you actually keep after tax and fees.

For a savings account, the rate less anything your bank charges. For a fund, the quoted return with its management fee named. For a Treasury Bill, the rate less 10%.

Then put that net number next to inflation at 15.39%.

That comparison — after everything, against rising prices — is the only one that tells you whether your money is actually growing. Almost nobody makes it, which is how people spend years feeling responsible while quietly losing ground.
