FGN Bonds, and the market nobody mentions

The government’s main borrowing. The auction wants ₦50 million — but you can own the same bonds from about ₦10,000, and that is worth understanding.
Once a month the Federal Government borrows a trillion naira or so in a single morning. Pension funds, banks and insurers bid, a rate is set, and that rate quietly decides what your savings account pays, what your bank charges for a loan, and what every other investment in the country has to beat. Most Nigerians have never heard of it.

The big one

An FGN Bond is the same idea as a savings bond, at a different scale and for a longer time: the government borrows, pays a fixed coupon twice a year, and returns your money at the end. The terms run from about five years to thirty.

The difference that matters is who it is designed for. The minimum bid at the auction is ₦50 million, so the room is full of institutions.

Why should you care about a room you are not in? Because the rate set in that room is the benchmark. When the September 2026 auction of a new 10-year bond cleared at 16.79%, that number became the reference point every bank, broker and fund manager in the country prices against.

What the auction rate tells you

If the government pays 16.79% to borrow for 10 years, then: · A company must offer more, because it is riskier than the government · Your bank can offer you less, and still profit · Any investment promising far more owes you an explanation It is the floor the whole market is measured from.

The secondary market — how you get in for ₦10,000

Here is the part that surprises people. A bond does not have to be bought at the auction. Once it exists, it can be sold on, from one owner to the next, for the rest of its life. That resale market is called the secondary market, and it is where an ordinary person buys.

The pension fund that bought ₦500 million of a bond in September can sell you ₦50,000 of it in March. You then own that slice, with the same coupon, from the same government, for the rest of the term. Brokers and investment apps do this routinely, often from about ₦10,000.

So the auction is a door most people cannot walk through, and the secondary market is the one standing open beside it.

Two ways to own the same bond

At the auction → ₦50,000,000 minimum, once a month, institutions only On the secondary market → from about ₦10,000, any working day, through a broker or app Same bond. Same government. Same coupon.

Why a bond’s price moves — the idea most people never get told

Buying second-hand introduces something the savings bond lesson did not have: a price that moves. And it moves for a reason worth understanding, because it explains most of what you will ever read about bond markets.

Suppose you own a bond paying 16% and rates in the market rise to 20%. Nobody will pay full price for your 16% bond when new ones pay 20% — so to sell, you must drop your price. Your bond is still perfectly safe and still pays 16% of its face value; it is simply worth less to a buyer today.

Now run it the other way. You own a bond paying 16% and rates fall to 12%. Suddenly yours is the good one, and buyers will pay above face value to get it.

So: when rates go up, bond prices go down. When rates go down, bond prices go up. It looks backwards until you see it from the buyer's side, and then it is obvious.

None of this touches you if you hold to maturity. You get your coupons and your money back as promised. Price only matters on the day you sell.

A ₦100,000 bond paying 16%

Market rates rise to 20% → to sell, you might accept ₦88,000 Market rates fall to 12% → a buyer might pay ₦115,000 You hold to the end instead → you receive exactly ₦100,000, as agreed The last line is the one to remember.

Tax, and when this suits you

Tax: none. Like the savings bond, interest on Federal and State government bonds is fully exempt under the Nigeria Tax Act 2025. A 16% government bond and a 16% corporate bond are not the same offer, because 10% comes off the corporate one.

When it suits you: money you genuinely will not need for years, where you want a known income and are happy to ignore the price in between. A ten-year bond paying a fixed coupon is a quiet, boring thing to own, and that is the point.

When it does not: if you might need to sell early, a long bond is the one most exposed to rate moves. The longer the remaining term, the more its price swings. If that worries you, the savings bond at two or three years, or a Treasury Bill at under a year, does the same job with far less movement.

Check yourself

You own a ₦500,000 FGN Bond paying a fixed 16%. Interest rates across the country rise sharply. You do not sell. What has happened to the money you will receive?

  • It falls, because the bond is now worth less
  • Nothing changes — you still get 16% and your ₦500,000 at the end
  • It rises, because rates went up
Show the answer

Right, and this is the distinction that trips up almost everyone. The bond's *price* fell, so it would fetch less if you sold it today. But the *contract* did not change: ₦80,000 a year, and your ₦500,000 at the end. A falling price is only a real loss if you sell into it.

Do this now

Open the next FGN Bond auction, linked below. You will not be bidding — look at the rate it clears at instead.

Then hold that number next to two things: the rate your savings account pays, and the rate on anything anyone has ever pitched to you. The government's borrowing rate is the honest middle of the Nigerian market. Anything well below it deserves a question, and anything well above it deserves a harder one.
