Alternatives — farms, property and co-ops

Where the biggest promises in Nigeria live, and where the most money has been lost. Both of those are true at once, and this is how to tell them apart.
Everything in this stage so far is traded somewhere — an exchange, an auction, a licensed fund. Alternatives are what is left: a farm cycle, a block of flats, a co-operative on your street. They are where the most exciting numbers in Nigeria are quoted, and where the most money has quietly disappeared.

Outside the market

An alternative investment is anything outside the listed market. The three that matter here:

Agricultural crowdfunding. You fund one farming cycle — maize, poultry, fish — and are promised a share of the harvest, usually quoted as a flat return over six to nine months.

Property crowdfunding. A platform pools money to buy or build, and pays you from the rent or the eventual sale.

Co-operatives, ajo and esusu. The oldest and least discussed. A group saves together, and members take turns receiving the pot, or the society lends to its own members.

What unites them: no exchange, no daily price, and usually no way out before the end.

How they differ from everything before

A bond: the government owes you, on a date, in writing. A share: you own part of a business you can sell any day. An alternative: a contract with one company, or one group of people, that you cannot sell to anyone else.

The record, which nobody puts in the advert

This is the part that decides whether you should take part, so it comes before the mechanics rather than after.

Agricultural crowdfunding grew fast in Nigeria and then ran into real trouble. The best-known platform needed $56 million of emergency funding in 2021 to keep going after it could not meet payouts. Others simply stopped paying and went quiet. Investors in those cycles had no exchange to sell on, no deposit insurance, and in many cases no licensed entity to pursue.

The lesson is not "never" — farming is real and people did get paid for years. The lesson is that a promised return is only as good as the company promising it, and these are small companies promising a lot.

And the structural problem has not gone away: a farm cycle fails for weather, disease, theft or a bad market, and when it does there is nothing underneath. A government bond has the full faith of the federal government behind it. A maize cycle has maize.

What the same promise looks like from both sides

"25% in nine months, agriculture-backed." What you are told: 25%. What you are taking on: one company, one crop, one season, no exit, no regulator standing behind it. The government pays 14.071% with none of that.

How to judge one, in four questions

Is it registered with the SEC? Since the crowdfunding rules came in, raising money from the public this way requires a registered portal. Check sec.gov.ng. Being registered does not make it safe; not being registered ends the conversation.

Who exactly owes you the money, and what do they own? A named limited company with assets is different from an app with a logo. Ask for the entity name and look it up.

What happens if the cycle fails? Insurance, a guarantee, or nothing? "Fully insured" is a claim — ask by whom, and for what.

When do I get it back, and what if I need it sooner? Usually: at the end, and you cannot. Treat the money as gone until the date.

Co-operatives are judged differently, because the counterparty is people you know. The questions become: is it registered, who holds the money, who has defaulted before, and what happens if someone takes the pot and leaves.

Where it fits, if it fits

Tax. Returns from these are ordinary income, with no exemption of the kind government bonds carry. Many platforms do not withhold anything, which leaves the obligation with you.

Getting out early. You cannot. There is no secondary market for a farm cycle or a crowdfunded flat. Assume the money is locked until the stated date, and that the date may slip.

How much. If you take part at all, the honest rule is money you could lose entirely without changing your life — and never the emergency fund, never school fees, never the deposit you promised someone. This is the opposite end of the ladder from the FGN Savings Bond, and it should be the smallest part of what you own, not the largest.

The thing worth remembering: the gap between 14.071% from the government and 25% from a farm is not free money. It is payment for the chance of getting nothing, and in this category that chance has been realised, repeatedly, within living memory.

Where it sits on the ladder

FGN Savings Bond → government owes you, fixed, exempt Corporate bond → a company owes you, rated, 10% tax Shares → you own a business, sell any day Alternatives → one company or group, no exit, no regulator behind it

Check yourself

A platform offers 28% over nine months on a poultry cycle, "fully insured". What is the first thing to establish?

  • Whether 28% beats the current bond rate
  • Whether the operator is registered with the SEC, and who legally owes you the money
  • How many people have already invested
Show the answer

Exactly. The rate is the last question, not the first — a number means nothing until you know who is obliged to pay it and whether they are allowed to take your money at all. "Fully insured" is a sentence anyone can type; ask who the insurer is and what event is covered.

Do this now

Think of the last agriculture or property investment someone showed you — there is almost always one. Find the company name in the small print, then search sec.gov.ng for it.

Most of the time you will not find it. That is the single most useful five minutes in this stage, and it costs nothing.
